Modernisation

Where automation stops and a person starts

Not every step of a process should be automated, and not every automated step should be done by a model. This is how to sort the steps, where to put the human review threshold, and how to move it later without anyone having to take it on faith.

Prints the one-page decision guide at the end of this document.

Start from the process, not the tool

Most automation programmes begin with a platform and then look for work to put on it. That ordering produces automation of the steps that were easiest to automate rather than the steps that were worth automating, and it is why so many of these programmes stall after the first three processes.

Map the process first, step by step, as it is actually performed. Then sort each step. Only then does the question of tooling arise, and by that point it mostly answers itself.

Three kinds of step

Every step in a business process falls into one of three categories, and the category determines what should implement it.

KindHow to recognise itWhat implements it
Deterministic Given the same input, the correct output is always the same, and the rule can be written down. Validation, arithmetic, routing on a documented condition, format conversion. Plain software. No model. A model here adds cost, latency and variance in exchange for nothing.
Judgement under a rule There is a right answer but reaching it needs reading, matching, or interpreting something unstructured. Matching an invoice to a purchase order, extracting fields from a document, classifying an exception. An agent or model, with a verifier around it and a confidence output.
Accountability Someone has to be answerable for the outcome. Approving a payment above a limit, accepting a risk, overriding a control, anything where a regulator would ask who decided. A person. Automation prepares the decision and never makes it.
The most expensive misclassification

Putting a model on a deterministic step because the platform makes it easy. It is slower, it costs per call, and it introduces variance into the one part of the process that had none. If you can write the rule down, write it down.

The threshold

For judgement steps the useful design is not automated or manual. It is a confidence threshold, with everything below it going to a person and everything above it proceeding on its own.

This only works if two things are true, and both are easy to get wrong.

  • The confidence figure has to mean something. A number a model emits about its own certainty is not a measurement. It has to be calibrated against known outcomes: of the items scored above the threshold, what proportion were actually right? Until you have that, you have a dial with no scale on it.
  • The review has to be real. A queue nobody has time for becomes a rubber stamp within a month, and a rubber stamp is worse than full automation because it creates the appearance of oversight without the substance.

Setting the first threshold

Start with everything going to review, and log the reviewer's decision next to the system's. That is a shadow period and it serves the same purpose as the parallel run: it produces the evidence before the risk is taken.

After enough volume you can read the agreement rate by confidence band, and set the threshold where the disagreement rate falls below what the process can tolerate. That tolerance is a business decision, and it differs enormously between a marketing classification and a payment release.

Moving it later

Thresholds should move as the system improves, and that movement is a controlled change with evidence behind it, not a configuration edit somebody makes on a Friday. What justifies a move:

  • Sustained agreement above the new threshold across a period covering the process cycle.
  • No change in the shape of the incoming work that would invalidate the history.
  • A named person who agreed to the new level, and the date they agreed.

Keep the ability to move it back, and make moving it back cheap. A threshold that can only go one direction will be defended past the point where it should have been lowered.

What every automated step leaves behind

The point of automating a governed process is not only that it runs without a person. It is that afterwards you can show what happened. Each automated step should record enough that somebody who was not there can reconstruct it:

  • What the input was, by reference.
  • What the step decided, and which implementation decided it.
  • The confidence, where the step was a judgement step.
  • Whether it crossed the threshold, and therefore whether a person saw it.
  • If a person saw it, who, when, and what they did.
  • Enough of the working to explain the outcome, without keeping so much that the record itself becomes a data protection problem.

That last balance is a real design decision and it is worth making deliberately. Keeping the full input of every step forever is the easy answer and it is frequently the wrong one.

Signs the threshold is in the wrong place

  • Reviewers agree with the system almost every time. The threshold is too low. You are paying people to confirm work that did not need confirming, and their attention is being spent where it earns nothing.
  • Reviewers overturn a meaningful share. Too high, or the model is not good enough for this step yet. Either way, lower it and find out which.
  • The queue is never empty. The threshold is set for a review capacity you do not have. This resolves as a rubber stamp unless someone changes it deliberately.
  • Nobody can say what the current threshold is. More common than it sounds, and it means the control is not being managed at all.

What not to automate, at any confidence

  • Anything where a regulation names a person as accountable. The automation prepares, the person decides, and the record shows both.
  • The final approval on an exception the system itself raised. A system that both flags and clears its own exceptions has no control in it.
  • Changes to the threshold itself. That is a control change and it belongs to a person.
