Modernisation

Mapping an estate before you touch it

Every modernisation starts with a map, and most maps are wrong because they record the design rather than the system. This is what to write down, how to find the parts nobody documented, and when to stop mapping and start work.

Prints the one-page field guide at the end of this document.

Why the existing diagram is not a map

Most organisations have an architecture diagram. It was drawn during a programme, it was accurate for about a quarter, and it describes the system somebody intended to build. The integrations added under deadline pressure are missing from it. So is the spreadsheet that three people depend on, the scheduled job on a machine under a desk, and the vendor feed that arrives by email.

Those omissions are not oversights. They are exactly the things that break a modernisation, because they are the dependencies nobody thinks to mention until they stop working.

A map is a record of what is running. It is built from evidence rather than from interviews alone, and it is finished when it can predict what breaks if you switch something off.

What to record

Six things per system. Fewer and the map cannot answer the questions you need it for. More and it will not get finished, which is the more common failure.

FieldWhat good looks like
What it doesOne sentence in business terms. If nobody can produce one, that is a finding in itself and it goes in the map.
Who owns itA named person for the business outcome, and a named person for the technology. Not a team, not a department. The parallel run will generate questions that only a person can settle.
What goes inEvery inbound interface, including the ones that are not interfaces: manual uploads, email attachments, a shared folder, someone rekeying from a screen.
What comes outSame rule. Files, API responses, reports, database writes another system reads directly, and anything a human copies out by hand.
What it runs onLanguage, framework, runtime version, host, and whether any of those are out of support. The out-of-support column is usually the one that unlocks the budget.
What happens if it stopsBlast radius in plain words, and how long the business can tolerate it being down. This is what turns a map into a priority order.

Finding what nobody will tell you

Interviews give you the systems people think about. The dependencies that break modernisations are the ones nobody thinks about, so they have to be found from evidence.

  • Read the network, not the diagram. Firewall rules, load balancer configuration and DNS records list things that are actually talking to each other. Entries nobody can explain are the most valuable rows in your map.
  • List the scheduled jobs. Cron tables, scheduler entries, and anything on a server that is not in the deployment pipeline. Batch is where undocumented behaviour lives.
  • Look at database accounts. Which accounts connect to a database, from where, tells you about direct reads that no interface document mentions. Cross-system direct database access is the single most common surprise.
  • Ask about the spreadsheets. Ask in the operations team, not in the architecture team, and ask it as "what do you have to do by hand every month". The answers are usually load-bearing.
  • Check the certificate and licence renewals. Anything with a renewal has an owner who pays for it, and that owner knows something.
The tell that a map is finished

You can pick any system and say, without checking, what breaks if it stops. If you cannot do that for a system, its inbound and outbound lists are still incomplete, whatever the diagram says.

Scoring risk without a ceremony

Risk scoring turns into a workshop industry very quickly. Two axes are enough to order the work, and both can be answered by the owner in a minute.

  • Consequence. If this stopped for a day, is it an inconvenience, a revenue event, or a regulatory event?
  • Fragility. Is it under support, does anyone still understand it, and when was it last changed successfully?

Something high on both is where the money is going to be spent eventually, and it is almost never the right first seam. Start somewhere with real pain and a small blast radius, prove the method, and come back to the frightening thing with a team that has already done it once.

How long this should take

For a mid-sized estate, one to three weeks. The map is a means to a decision, and the decision is which seam to take first. If mapping runs past a month it has become the project.

Stop when you can name the first seam and defend the choice. Everything else can be filled in during the work, and it will be more accurate then anyway, because you will be reading the system rather than asking about it.

What to resist

A tooling decision. Estate maps get delayed for months while a discovery platform is evaluated. A spreadsheet with the six columns above, filled in honestly, beats an empty tool every time, and it takes an afternoon to set up.

What the map is used for afterwards

It should not be a one-off artifact. Three ongoing uses justify keeping it current:

  • Choosing each next seam, since the map is the only place the blast radius is written down.
  • Answering audit and due-diligence questions, which arrive without notice and always ask what systems process a given category of data.
  • Decommissioning. The map is what tells you whether anything still depends on the thing you want to switch off.

Keeping it current is a small standing job, and it is worth attaching it to an existing ritual, such as a change advisory step, rather than creating a review nobody attends.

Finished when

You can pick any system and say, without checking, what breaks if it stops. And you can name the first seam and defend the choice. One to three weeks for a mid-sized estate. Past a month, the map has become the project.

Two things to resist

  • Evaluating a discovery tool first. Six honest columns in a spreadsheet beat an empty platform.
  • Starting at the frightening system. Prove the method somewhere with real pain and a small blast radius, then come back to it.
