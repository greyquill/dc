The discovery week

What one to two weeks of proper discovery should produce before anyone writes production code, what happens in each day of it, and how to tell whether the output is good enough to build from.

Prints the one-page agenda and checklist at the end.

What discovery is for

Discovery is not a requirements-gathering ritual and it is not a sales exercise. Its purpose is to convert an idea into three artifacts that a team can build from and a sponsor can fund, and to surface the disagreements that would otherwise appear in month three.

The most valuable thing it produces is usually not the specification. It is the list of questions nobody had asked yet, and the discovery that two stakeholders meant different things by the same word.

The three outputs

01

A scoped specification

What is being built, in enough detail to estimate, with what is explicitly out of scope written down beside it. The out-of-scope list is the half people skip and the half that prevents the argument later.

02

A prioritised backlog

Ordered by value and by what unblocks what, not by what is easiest. Each item has an acceptance criterion someone other than the author could check.

03

An estimate with its uncertainty stated

A range, with the assumptions it depends on and the things that would move it. A single number with no assumptions attached is a guess wearing a suit.

How the time is spent

A one-week shape for a contained scope. Two weeks where there are several stakeholder groups or an existing system to understand.

The questions that earn their place

Most of discovery's value comes from a small number of questions that people find slightly uncomfortable. These are the ones worth asking every time.

  • What happens if we do nothing? If the honest answer is "not much", that is worth knowing in week one rather than after the build.
  • Who has to change how they work for this to succeed? Software that requires a behaviour change from people who were not consulted fails regardless of its quality.
  • What has been tried before, and what happened? There is almost always a previous attempt. Understanding why it stalled is the cheapest research available.
  • Which of these requirements would you drop to ship two months earlier? Asked directly, it produces a real priority order in ten minutes.
  • Who signs it off, and what will they check? Knowing the acceptance test from day one changes what gets built.
  • Where does the data come from, and who owns it? Data availability is the most common cause of a plan that has to be rewritten in month two.

How to tell the output is good enough

Four tests. If a specification fails any of them, another day of discovery costs less than the rework it prevents.

  • The estimate test. Two engineers who were not in the room read it separately and produce estimates within the same range. Wildly different numbers mean it is being read differently, which means it is ambiguous.
  • The acceptance test. Every item has a criterion somebody other than its author could check without asking what was meant.
  • The out-of-scope test. There is a written list of what is not being built. An empty list means the boundary has not been drawn, not that everything is included.
  • The disagreement test. At least one real disagreement was found and resolved during the week. Discovery that surfaced no disagreement usually did not go deep enough to find one.

What discovery does not do

  • It does not remove uncertainty. It states it, so it can be priced and planned around.
  • It does not produce a fixed price for a vague problem. If the scope is genuinely unclear, the honest output is a smaller first phase rather than a bigger number.
  • It does not replace the first sprint. Some things are only learned by building, and a specification that claims otherwise is overreaching.
The failure mode

Discovery that runs long. Past two weeks it stops producing decisions and starts producing documents. If the questions cannot be closed in the time, the answer is a narrower first phase, not a longer discovery.

Four tests on the output

  • Estimate. Two engineers who were not in the room estimate within the same range.
  • Acceptance. Every item checkable by someone other than its author.
  • Out of scope. A written list exists. Empty means the boundary was never drawn.
  • Disagreement. At least one real one was found and resolved.
